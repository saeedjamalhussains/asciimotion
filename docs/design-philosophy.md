# Raster Ledger

*A design philosophy for ASCII Motion's interface.*

Raster Ledger treats the screen as an instrument, not a surface. Every element sits on an
invisible character grid, a field of identical cells, the same lattice the renderer pours
light into. Space is counted, not guessed. Margins are whole multiples of a single unit,
borders are one hairline wide, and nothing is rounded beyond the two pixels a pixel needs to
feel cut rather than torn. The composition should read like a measured drawing: deliberate,
quiet, and so evenly spaced that the eye finds no seam to catch on. That evenness is the
product of painstaking attention. It is meant to look as though a master draughtsman ruled
every line twice.

Colour is rationed like current. The ground is near-black with the faintest green cast, the
colour of a cathode tube at rest. One phosphor green carries every signal: what is active,
what is selected, where the playhead stands. One amber marks the range the user has cut, and
that is the only second voice. Everything else lives in a tight ladder of greys, each step
chosen so that hierarchy comes from luminance alone. No gradients and no glows decorate the
chrome. Light belongs to the artwork, and the frame around it stays dark so the artwork stays
the brightest thing in the room. The palette should feel calibrated, not picked: the result
of countless small adjustments until nothing competes.

Type is monospaced throughout, so the interface speaks the same language as its output.
Labels are small, uppercase and widely tracked, whispered rather than announced. Figures use
tabular digits and sit in fixed columns so values never jitter as they change. Sections carry
index numbers, 01 to 08, like the plates of a technical atlas, and the stage carries corner
registration marks and a quiet read-out of its own dimensions. These are reference marks, not
decoration. They imply that what happens on the stage is being observed and measured with
expert care.

Rhythm comes from repetition. Ticks along the timeline gather into a dense ruler, progress
fills in discrete blocks instead of a smooth bar, and switches and slider thumbs are squares
that step rather than glide. Motion is brief and mechanical: a control answers the pointer at
once, a press settles one pixel down, a panel opens in a short ease and then holds still.
Nothing pulses for attention and nothing moves unless the user moved it. Under reduced motion
everything simply rests. Each transition is tuned so it is felt more than seen.

The hierarchy is strict and shallow. The stage dominates, the transport floats beneath it as
a single ruled strip, and the controls stand in a narrow column of numbered plates. Two boxes
deep is the limit, and one button style, one field style and one border weight serve
everywhere. The final work should look meticulously crafted, the product of deep expertise,
as if each alignment had been refined until it could not be improved. Restraint is the method
and precision is the ornament.
